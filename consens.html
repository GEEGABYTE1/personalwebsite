Research article   EEG microcircuit emulation

Emulating EEG from Human Cortical Microcircuits

A two-model pipeline built on the Yao et al. (2022) human layer 2/3 microcircuit. Model 1 simulates scalp EEG from a 23-dimensional microcircuit configuration and generates a synthetic (config, EEG) dataset; Model 2 is a neural network that learns the config → EEG map directly, replacing the ~15-minute biophysical simulation with a sub-second forward pass.

GitHub / source

Central result: the microcircuit configuration is encoded chiefly in the EEG amplitude (~10× dynamic range with excitatory drive), and the learned emulator recovers this on held-out configurations at Pearson r ≈ 0.74.

00   Abstract

Detailed biophysical models of cortical microcircuits reproduce EEG-scale signals but are expensive to simulate, limiting their use for inference and large-scale exploration. We build two complementary models on the Yao et al. human L2/3 microcircuit. Model 1 is a generator: it takes a microcircuit configuration, applies biologically-constrained evolutionary perturbations, runs a full NEURON/LFPy forward simulation with active ion channels and recurrent spiking, and computes a true scalp EEG through a current-dipole four-sphere head model. Model 2 is a neural-network emulator trained on the resulting (config, EEG) pairs to predict EEG directly from the 23-D configuration. Our central empirical finding is that the configuration is encoded primarily in EEG amplitude (a ~10× dynamic range as excitatory drive varies), and that the emulator recovers this on held-out configurations (r ≈ 0.74).

01   Background

Yao et al. (2022, Cell Reports) built data-driven models of human cortical L2/3 microcircuits — 1,000 neurons across four cell types (Pyramidal 80%, SST 5%, PV 7%, VIP 8%) with human-fit morphologies and ion-channel biophysics, simulated in NEURON + LFPy — and showed that reduced somatostatin (SST) inhibition, as in depression, raises baseline activity and lowers the cortical signal-to-noise ratio.

That model is a forward simulator. This project turns it into (i) a data generator producing plausible microcircuit variants and their EEG, and (ii) a fast learned surrogate of the simulation — a step toward using such circuits to study EEG biomarkers and, eventually, to invert EEG back to microcircuit state.

02   Overview

The 23-D configuration parameterises the biologically-tunable knobs of the column: [4 background-drive scales, 3 synaptic-weight scales, 16 connection-probability scales]. Model 1 maps a config to an EEG trace by simulation; Model 2 learns to short-circuit that map.

03   Model 1 — Microcircuit to EEG generator

Microcircuit and biophysics

A scaled 100-neuron L2/3 column preserving Yao's cell-type ratios (Pyr 80 / SST 5 / PV 7 / VIP 8). Each neuron is a multi-compartment model built from the original Yao morphologies and hoc templates, with the human L2/3 active ion channels (NaTg, Kv3.1, CaHVA/CaLVA, SK, Ih, Im, KP/KT, Nap) compiled and inserted — so cells genuinely spike, with correct type signatures (fast-spiking PV, regular-spiking Pyr).

Background drive and recurrence

Each cell receives an excitatory Ornstein–Uhlenbeck background conductance, calibrated per cell type to in-vivo baseline rates. Recurrent connectivity is realised by a two-pass spike-injection scheme, which keeps the memory-safe one-cell-at-a-time simulation while approximating the closed loop:

  1. Pass 1 — simulate each cell with OU drive only; record its somatic spike times.
  2. Build a global registry of presynaptic spike trains.
  3. Pass 2 — re-simulate each cell, driving its synapses with the recorded spike trains of its presynaptic partners (at the mapped conduction delays). This pass produces the EEG.

From membrane currents to scalp EEG

Per cell we accumulate the net current-dipole moment, summed across the column to give the population dipole, then project it to a scalp electrode through a four-shell (brain, CSF, skull, scalp) volume-conductor head model. This yields a true EEG (not intracortical LFP), band-limited to the physiological <45 Hz range.

Evolutionary perturbation

Configs are generated by an evolutionary search over the 23-D space under biological-plausibility rules (box bounds per parameter class and an excitation/inhibition-balance constraint), with a fitness that rewards plausibility and novelty. This yields a diverse population of realistic microcircuit variants, each then simulated (in parallel, with resumable checkpointing) to attach an EEG trace.

04   Model 2 — Neural forward emulator

A PyTorch network maps the normalised 23-D config to an EEG waveform: a fully-connected stack projects the config to a seed sequence, which a 1-D transposed-convolution decoder upsamples to the output trace. Training minimises a spectral loss on the log-magnitude Fourier spectrum (which targets the reproducible structure) plus a small time-domain term.

ComponentChoice
Input23-D config, log-normalised to [0,1]
DecoderFC (23 to 128 to 128) then 1-D conv-transpose then interpolate to T
Losslog-spectral MSE + 0.2 × time-domain MSE
Normalisationglobal (dataset-level) — preserves config-dependent amplitude

Why global (not per-trace) normalisation? Per-trace z-scoring forces every EEG to unit power, erasing exactly the amplitude differences that encode the configuration (see Findings). Dataset-level normalisation keeps relative amplitude, so the emulator can learn it.

06   Key findings

  • The inherited cells were silently passive. The original code referenced ion channels whose compiled mechanisms were missing (inside try/except: pass), so nothing spiked. Compiling the Yao channels and building from their templates was the prerequisite for everything else.
  • True EEG needs the dipole path. The prior pipeline stopped at intracortical LFP; adding the current-dipole four-sphere projection yields an actual scalp EEG.
  • The simulated spontaneous EEG is high-frequency dominated at 100 cells (spike transients do not average out as they would in a ~106-neuron column), so a physiological <45 Hz band-limit is applied — matching how the skull filters real scalp EEG.
  • Config information lives in amplitude. After band-limiting and per-trace normalisation the <45 Hz spectral shape is nearly config-invariant; the config signal is in the overall amplitude. Saving raw amplitude and using global normalisation recovers it (r ≈ 0.74).

07   Reproduce

Requires the project virtual-env (Python 3.10; LFPy, NEURON, PyTorch). The active ion channels must be compiled once.

1 · Compile ion-channel mechanisms

cd mechanisms
CPLUS_INCLUDE_PATH="$(xcrun --show-sdk-path)/usr/include/c++/v1" nrnivmodl .

2 · Model 1 generate the (config, EEG) dataset

export PYTHONPATH=$PWD
VPY=../venv/bin/python

# raw-amplitude dataset (36 configs, 0.5 s sims, 8 in parallel)
$VPY model1_microcircuit_generator.py --simulate --raw \
     --generations 20 --population 40 --max_configs 36 \
     --tstop 500 --trim_ms 50 --workers 8 --seed 200 \
     --out data/model1_eeg_raw

3 · Model 2 train the emulator

$VPY model2_emulator.py --data data/model1_eeg_raw.h5 \
     --norm global --hidden 128 --epochs 300 \
     --out models/model2_emulator_global.pt

4 · Amplitude experiment (Figure 2)

$VPY experiments/amplitude_test.py --workers 8

Forward simulations are ~7–15 min each (100 cells); runs are parallelised and checkpointed (resumable). Full documentation in the repository README.md.

08   Limitations and next steps

  • Small training set. Model 2's amplitude head was trained on 36 configs and regresses toward the mean; more samples would sharpen it (the generator is parallel and checkpointed for this).
  • Spontaneous spectral shape is config-weak. In the physiological band the <45 Hz shape barely moves with the config, so most learnable signal is amplitude.
  • Evoked paradigm is the natural next target. A stimulus-evoked, time-locked EEG deflection is config-dependent and directly learnable as a waveform (raise the emulator's time-domain loss weight) — promising given experimental data is largely stimulus-evoked.
